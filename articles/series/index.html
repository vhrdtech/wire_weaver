Basic

Everything Series: from one line to the bit layout¶

Roman Isaikin, 18 October 2026

A motor controller measures its phase current 25 000 times a second. Written one value after another, 32 of those readings with their timestamps take 321 bytes. As a Series, the same 32 readings take 34. Nothing is thrown away, nothing is rounded, the reader gets back exactly what the device measured: the bytes were redundancy all along.

This article is the whole story of Series<T> in WireWeaver, in three parts. Basic is what it is, the one line that uses it and what it saves, including batched streams and observing every sample. Advanced is every knob: codec policies, quantities and autoranging, how it compares with ALP and with raw globals, sealing, budgets, the shared clock, Python, what it costs on a microcontroller, and how far it goes at a power converter's 400 kHz to 2 MHz. Nomicon is how the bits are laid out and how the encoder got six times faster. Read until you have what you need; the numbers below were measured on the running code (the one worked out from the layout says so), and each part links to the reference pages for the details.

Basic

Basic: sequences that shrink¶

What a Series is, the line that uses it, what it saves, and how streams and observed properties batch into one.

Values over time¶

Most of what a device sends is a sequence of values over time: currents, voltages, temperatures, counters, encoder positions, timestamps. Consecutive values are close to each other, a timestamp grows by the same step every time, and the unit or range of a reading almost never changes. A Vec<T> doesn't know any of that: it writes every item in full.

Series<'i, T> is a list of T that does know. It writes the list column by column instead of item by item: all the timestamps, then all the units, then all the values, each column compressed on its own with the code that fits it best. A timestamp ticking at a fixed rate can cost nothing per item; a unit that doesn't change costs about 5 bits for the whole batch; a slowly moving reading costs a few bits per item instead of 4 bytes.

It is still shrink_wrap: zero-copy reading, no_std and no allocation on the device, and it evolves the way T does (a field appended to T later is skipped by an older reader).

One line¶

Mark the item type with series and write the slice:

use shrink_wrap::prelude::*;
use shrink_wrap::series::{Series, SeriesOwned};
use ww_si::{Ampere, Second, Timed};

let items: Vec<Timed<Ampere>> = /* 32 currents with their times */;
let bytes = Series::new(&items).to_ww_bytes_owned()?; // that's the line

let back: Vec<Timed<Ampere>> = Series::<Timed<Ampere>>::from_ww_bytes(&bytes)?.iter().collect();
let owned = SeriesOwned::<Timed<Ampere>>::from_ww_bytes_owned(&bytes)?; // std: straight into a Vec

ww_si quantities and Timed<T> already have series; your own types get it in their derive:

#[derive_shrink_wrap(borrowed, owned, series)]
struct IvSample { v: Volt, i: Ampere }

A Series goes anywhere a Vec would: a method argument or return value, a property, a stream item, a field of another struct.

#[ww_trait]
trait Sequences {
    /// Voltage history with device time
    fn history() -> Series<'i, Timed<Volt>>;
    property!(rw stamps: Series<'i, u64>);
}

Structs, enums with or without data, Option, all integers, floats, bool, tuples and arrays are in, nested to any depth, and so are strings and byte lists, written as runs; which T has the exact list and the few types that aren't yet. One limit for strings: a Series carries them anywhere a Vec would, but a batched stream or observed property needs a size bound per item, and observe cells a fixed-size cell per value, which a string doesn't have (see strings and byte lists below).

What it saves¶

The 32 currents from the top of the page: a motor phase current in mA as I32, around 5 A on a slow sine with a few mA of noise, one sample every 40 µs:

32 × Timed<Ampere> bytes per sample
Vec<Timed<Ampere>> 321 10.0
Series, current stored as f32 104 3.3
Series, current as I32 mA 34 1.1

On recorded test signals (IvSample above, a voltage and a current, 100 items):

100 items Vec<T> smallest plain form Series
steady (±1 mV, ±2 µA) 902 B 802 B 104 B
noisy (slow sine + noise) 902 B 802 B 251 B
range switches (µA ↔ mA for 40 items) 902 B 802 B 110 B

3 to 8 times smaller is the rule of thumb: 7.7x on a steady signal, 3.2x on a noisy one. A single item costs one byte more than plain (the column headers are paid once), two items are already smaller, and most of the gain is there by 32 items per batch, which is why 32 is the default batch size everywhere below.

Quantities: integers with a prefix¶

One piece of advice comes out of every measurement: store integers with a prefix, not floats. A steady f32 voltage (100 values) is 136 B; the same signal as millivolts in a U16 is 45 B (noisy: 277 B against 82 B). The prefix sets the resolution, and noise below the resolution costs nothing; an f32 pays for every mantissa bit the ADC's noise touches.

That is exactly what a ww_si quantity is: an SI prefix and a number, usually an integer (a firmware typically enables I32, U32 and F32 storage). Ampere::milli(4980) is 4 980 mA, and the unit itself takes no bits. In a Series the prefix and the storage type are runs that cost a few bits per batch when they don't change, and the integer column carries only the changes.

A device may also change the prefix on the fly: µA while a current is small, mA once it grows. This is autoranging, and it costs almost nothing: at a prefix change the column starts over with a full value instead of a meaningless delta across the jump, and goes on compressing in the new range. On the motor demo's currents, ranging between µA and mA switched 528 times a second and still came out a little smaller than staying in µA (details in the Advanced part).

Batched streams: one word¶

You rarely build a Series by hand for a live signal. Mark a stream batched and the device collects items into batches for you:

#[ww_trait]
trait Sensors {
    #[batched]
    stream!(readings: Sample);
}

On the device, push items wherever they come from, an interrupt included; the push is O(1) and takes no lock:

let ser = api_impl::stream_data_ser();
ser.readings_push(&mut self.readings_stream, sample, t);          // one item with its time
ser.readings_push_many(&mut self.readings_stream, t0, dt, block); // a DMA block at once

On the host, items arrive one at a time, each with its own time, exactly as from a stream that isn't batched, or a whole batch at once with recv_batch(). The API has no batch size: the device owns it (32 by default), and the host picks only the latency, .max_age(duration), how long a batch may wait before it goes. Details: Batching streams and properties.

Observing every sample¶

Observing a property sends its current value at the rate a host asks for. A value that changes faster than that, a current sampled in a 25 kHz control loop, loses everything in between. Add +batched and a host can ask for every sample:

#[ww_trait]
trait Foc {
    property!(ro+observe+batched i_a: Ampere);
    property!(ro+observe+batched i_b: Ampere);
}

The device hands over each new set of values once per control loop tick, and the host gets every one of them:

// device, in the control loop
server.observe.samplers().root.sample(Second::micro(now_us), api_impl::FocSamples { i_a, i_b });

// host
let mut i_a = device.foc().observe_i_a(samples(Duration::from_millis(5))).subscribe().await?;
while let Ok(s) = i_a.recv().await {
    println!("{:?} {:?}", s.t, s.value); // every sample, oldest first, each with its device time
}

A host may observe just a part of a level: here i_a alone, or 3 of the 21 values of the motor demo's FOC level. Only the properties in Samples mode get a column in the level's batch, are encoded and go on the wire. What doesn't shrink is the capture: the interrupt still hands over (or, with observe cells, writes) every value of the row. How much that is: a few properties of a level.

The motor demo, in numbers¶

The motor demo is a simulated FOC motor controller with about 50 observable properties in three rate tiers. Its foc level has 21 values computed every 40 µs (25 kHz), control has 13 measured values at 1 kHz. Observing every single sample of a level, through the real generated code:

every sample foc (21 values, 25 kHz) control (13 values, 1 kHz)
one update per sample, not batched 3.73 MB/s 99.0 kB/s
batched, 32 samples per batch 812 kB/s 19.0 kB/s
smaller by 4.6x 5.2x

The flip side: unbatched, a ww_si quantity is a little bigger than a bare f32 (it carries its prefix and storage type): the demo's foc level observed every millisecond went from 96 to 117 kB/s when it moved to quantities. Quantities pay off inside a Series, where a value costs its change from the previous one, not its width.

Advanced

Advanced: every knob, and what it costs¶

The choices behind a batch: which codecs, when it seals, how big it may get, what it costs a microcontroller, and when to leave batching off.

Codec policies: smallest, fixed, or fixed at compile time¶

Each numeric column is written with a predictor (raw, delta, delta of delta) and a coder (prefix code, bit packed, constant, plain); floats are plain or XOR coded. Who picks them is the SeriesPolicy:

  • CodecPolicy::SMALLEST (the default of Series::new, and of everything on std) tries every combination per column and keeps the smallest.
  • CodecPolicy::fixed(numeric, float) uses one for every column, chosen at run time.
  • A type implementing FixedCodecs fixes the codecs at compile time and links only them. DeltaPacked (delta, packed residuals, XOR floats) is one, and the no_std default of a batcher. RawPlain is another.
#[derive(Clone, Copy)]
struct DeltaOfDeltaPacked;

impl FixedCodecs for DeltaOfDeltaPacked {
    const NUMERIC: NumericCodec = NumericCodec::new(Predictor::DeltaOfDelta, Coder::Packed);
    const FLOAT: FloatCodec = FloatCodec::Xor;
}

Series::with_policy(&items, DeltaOfDeltaPacked).to_ww_bytes(&mut buf)?;

All of them write the same bytes for the same codecs; the reader follows the column headers, whatever wrote them. What the choice costs, encoding 32 samples of a motor phase current (Series<Timed<Ampere>>) on a Cortex-M3:

policy instructions per item bytes encoder flash
SMALLEST 969 50 25.4 KB
fixed(delta, packed) 341 79 25.4 KB
fixed(delta-of-delta, packed) 334 61 25.4 KB
DeltaPacked 341 79 15.0 KB

So a fixed codec is about 3x cheaper in CPU than trying them all, for 1.2x to 1.7x the bytes. A run-time CodecPolicy value links every codec whatever its value; only a FixedCodecs type leaves the rest out. And RawPlain is not the CPU escape hatch it looks like: on an STM32H725 a level batch takes 567 k cycles with it against 529 k with DeltaPacked, for 2.8x the bytes; writing more bits costs more than predicting saves.

Where the fixed codecs lose most is a regular clock. SMALLEST writes it as delta + constant, zero bits per item; DeltaPacked has no constant coder and pays a packed residual per tick. The 32 currents from the Basic part are 34 B with SMALLEST and 65 B with DeltaPacked; without their times, 27 B and 30 B. That gap is what the shared clock of observe levels (below) takes away.

Quantities, autoranging and restarts¶

A ww_si quantity is a final_structure struct { prefix: Prefix, value: NumericValue }, so a Series of quantities has three columns per quantity: the prefix, the storage type of the number (I32, U32, F32, ..), and the number itself, in one column per storage type that occurs.

Discrete leaves, enum tags like the prefix and the storage type, bool, Option presence and strings, are written as runs: the first value, then one entry per change. A value that never changes in a batch costs about 5 bits in all; a string costs its bytes once per change, which suits a status text, less a log line that changes every item. Measured on the motor demo, the prefix and storage runs of a quantity and its timestamp cost 3 B per series per update, 5 % of the bytes at 32 items: not worth a fixed-prefix wire form that would give up autoranging.

A numeric column restarts (a full value, fresh codec state) where an enum field declared earlier in the same struct changed. Ampere { prefix, value } restarts value when the prefix changes, so 480 µA followed by 120 mA is a full value, not a meaningless delta of -360 across two units. A switch costs a run entry, one full value instead of a residual and a packed block cut short: about 6 B, worked out from the layout.

What that means in practice was measured on the motor demo's simulated FOC level (21 values at 25 kHz, 7 of them currents, every sample, 32 per batch, an early prototype of batched observe with a time column per property and SMALLEST), with the 7 currents stored three ways:

currents bytes/s of the level prefix switches/s
fixed mA 913.1 kB/s 0
fixed µA 1 126.7 kB/s 0
µA below 1 A, mA above (autoranging) 1 114.4 kB/s 528

Autoranging between µA and mA switched 528 times a second and came out 12.3 kB/s below a fixed µA range: the switches pay for themselves through the coarser range above 1 A. It still costs about what fixed µA costs, because 91 to 100 % of the current samples were below 1 A and were sent at µA resolution. Resolution decides the bytes, not switching: pick the prefix for the resolution you need, and let the device range freely where its measurement range does.

Compared with ALP¶

ALP (Afroozeh, Kuffó, Boncz: ALP: Adaptive Lossless floating-Point Compression, SIGMOD 2024) is the state of the art for compressing doubles in analytical databases. It turns doubles that started out as decimals into integers: for each vector of 1 024 values it finds an exponent e and a factor f (by sampling, first per row group, then per vector) so that round(n × 10^e × 10^-f) decodes back to exactly n. Values that don't round-trip are stored as exceptions (64 bits plus a 16-bit position each), the integers are bit packed with a frame of reference, and doubles that aren't decimals go to a second scheme, ALPrd, which compresses their front bits.

A ww_si quantity does ALP's first step at the producer. The device already holds an integer (an ADC count scaled to mA, a time in µs), and the SI prefix is the decimal exponent, in steps of 10³ (with deci, centi, deca and hecto around the unit). So there is no search, no exceptions, it is lossless by construction, and the exponent may change per sample, where ALP keeps one per vector. What ALP has beyond that, a scale chosen from the data per block and at any power of ten, is what the planned common factor column mode would add: the predictor code 11 is kept free as an escape for it (see Ahead).

The store and the batcher¶

A device collects items in a SeriesStore, which series derives next to the encoder: room for N items as a struct of arrays, one array per leaf field, no padding between them. Timed<Ampere> takes 12 bytes per item there, against 24 in a [Timed<Ampere>; 32].

A batched stream's or observe level's Batcher holds two such stores: the producer (an interrupt, the control loop) fills one while the event loop encodes the other, and sealing swaps them behind one atomic word, no lock. On a Cortex-M3 a push is about 62 instructions, the push that seals a batch 71, a poll that seals nothing 108. If the spare store isn't back from encoding when the next batch seals, that batch is lost and counted, it never blocks the producer. RAM for the motor demo's whole foc level at 32 samples: 8.4 KB. SeriesStore and the store have the per-type layouts.

When the batch is encoded, each column is written straight from its array in the store, one pass per column, with the same bytes as walking the items. A batch in which a prefix or another tag changes falls back to the item walk, so autoranging still works, at the walk's speed for that batch.

Observe cells: capture as cheap as a global¶

sample(t, FocSamples { .. }) takes a level's row as one value: the interrupt builds all 21 values on its stack, and the sampler copies them into the batch. For a level sampled in a hot interrupt, a device can opt into observe cells instead, per level, in ww_codegen!: a ring of atomic cells the interrupt writes in place, each value where it is computed.

// ww_codegen!(motor_demo_api::MotorDemo for Device, server = true, .., observe_cells = "root_foc")
static FOC: api_impl::RootFocCells<32> = api_impl::RootFocCells::new(); // const fn, a ring of 32 rows

// the interrupt keeps the writer, handed out once
let mut w = FOC.writer().unwrap();
// .. every PWM period, where the values are computed
w.i_a(Ampere::milli(ia));
w.i_b(Ampere::milli(ib));
w.commit(Second::micro(now_us)); // publishes the row if a host wants it

Each setter is a few relaxed stores, typed by the API; commit publishes the row between two release fences. No lock, no read-modify-write, no unsafe in user code, and the writer can't be cloned, so there is one producer by construction. poll_properties copies the committed rows into one staging batch and encodes it as before. A row the interrupt overwrote before it was read is dropped and counted in missed, never sent half old, half new.

The API and the wire don't change: same columns, same bytes, a host can't tell which one a device uses. On the STM32H725 a row of the 21 FOC values takes 342 cycles at opt-level = 3 against 423 through sample(), and 357 against 1 400 at opt-level = "z"; its stack drops from 116-388 B to 44-52 B. RAM is the same, 8.6 KB for the FOC level with a ring of 32 rows. The copy moves to the event loop, so total CPU stays about the same; what changes is that the interrupt is short and predictable. Under backpressure cells even lose fewer samples: rows wait in the ring while a sealed batch waits for budget. Observing: cells has the setup, the Nomicon the numbers.

Sealing and budgets¶

A batch is sealed for one of four reasons: it is full (N items), one more item could pass the event's byte budget, it got as old as the host allows, or the code flushed it (on closing a stream).

The budget needs to know how big a batch can get without writing it. Series::<T>::max_bytes(n) is the worst case under any policy, every column at its longest code and every run changing at every item, and it is far from real data: the 32 currents above are 34 B against a bound of 1 272 B (with every number type of NumericValue enabled). So the batcher starts from the bound and then seals by the last batch's measured bytes per item, aiming at 80 % of the budget. A batch that still turns out too big is split, the largest prefix that fits goes now and the rest next time: nothing lost, nothing sent twice. See sealing and budget.

One clock per observe level¶

The batched properties of an observe level are sampled together, by one sample(t, ..) call, so they share their times. A level batch is one Series<T> per observed property plus one Series<Second> of times in the update's head (sample_times), not a time column per property. On the foc level's 21 properties at 32 samples with DeltaPacked, that is 1 251 B against 1 965 B (36 % less) and half the encode cycles. Through the real motor demo with SMALLEST, which already wrote each regular clock almost for free, it still saved 17 % (foc) and 26 % (control).

Two things follow. One batcher per level seals the level's properties together: control went from 238 updates/s, sealed out of step property by property, to 48. And a level's Samples entries share one grid and one age: they get the finest every_us and the shortest max_age_us any of them asked for, and the grant says so. A property that needs its own sample times belongs in its own trait level; levels are the rate tiers.

A few properties of a level¶

Observing only some properties of a level is supported, and it is the usual case: a host asks for i_a, i_b and i_c in Samples mode and leaves the other 18 values of the FOC level alone. Only properties in Samples mode get a column in the level's batch, are encoded and sent. The others still cost CPU where the row is captured: sample() takes the whole row (FocSamples, all 21 values) and copies it into the batch store, and with observe cells the interrupt writes every cell. The poll copies only the observed columns out of the cells' ring. Measured on the STM32H725 at opt-level = 3, the FOC bench with the host observing all 21, then only i_a, i_b, i_c:

FOC level (21 values), H725 cycles all 21 observed 3 observed
a row, sample() 408 386
a row, observe cells 333 316
a poll that drains 16 rows from the cells' ring 10 100 - 10 300 4 370 - 4 460
the poll that encodes a 32-row batch, sample() 100 200 24 500 - 24 900
the same with cells (it also drains 16 rows) 107 400 26 200 - 26 300
bytes sent for 64 rows 2 021 419

So the encode, the cells' drain and the bytes follow what is observed (4x fewer cycles and 5x fewer bytes here), the capture doesn't: a row costs the same whether a host watches 3 values or 21. At 25 kHz that makes 3 of the 21 FOC values about 6 % of the H725 (rows plus polls, sample() or cells alike), against 17 % for all 21 through sample() and 19 % with cells. A property a host starts to observe while a batch fills still gets that batch's earlier samples, as with sample(): the poll copies its column for the rows already staged from the ring (if the interrupt wrote over them meanwhile, the batch goes without it and the next one has it). To make the unobserved values free, put the ones that are rarely watched in a level of their own (a level is the unit a gate opens for), or skip computing them when nobody samples the level at all (if w.is_sampling()).

Without generated code: CLI, dynamic clients, Python¶

A Series in an API is in its introspection data, so tools that only have that read and write it too. ww stream <path> --batches prints whole batches, ww observe --samples MS one line per sample. The dynamic client decodes items or columns, and it also writes a Series from a dynamic value with the same bytes the generated code gives (ValueOwned::ser_series_dyn, any policy). In Python a Series<T> is a list, so a host writes one as it would a list, and reads it as items or as numpy columns:

dev.stamps.write([1_700_000_000, 1_700_000_010, 1_700_000_020])  # property!(rw stamps: Series<'i, u64>)

cols = dev.readings.decode_columns(data)  # one numpy array per leaf field
with m.observe({"current": {"samples": 5}}) as obs:  # every sample, batches at most 5 ms old
    b = obs.recv_batch()
    b["batches"]["current"]               # {"t": array of seconds, "": array of the values}

Python: observing and values have the rest.

CPU and flash on a microcontroller¶

Pushing is cheap and flat; encoding is where the CPU goes, and it scales with samples per second (properties × rate). Two kinds of numbers below, in different units:

  • Cortex-M3/M4F: instructions, counted under QEMU (examples_mcu/qemu_icount). QEMU has no timing model, so these are not cycles and give no time on their own.
  • STM32H725 (Cortex-M7 at 540 MHz, caches on): cycles, read from the core's DWT cycle counter on the real chip. Here one QEMU instruction took about 2 to 2.5 cycles, so a bigger number for the M7 doesn't mean more work.

With DeltaPacked at 32 samples per batch, a timed stream item is about 341 instructions on a Cortex-M3/M4F and about 790 cycles on the H725, 1.46 µs per item. An observe level's sample costs about half, thanks to the shared clock: 213 instructions on a Cortex-M3/M4F. How that scales:

observed (one level, shared clock) samples/s Cortex-M3/M4F, instructions/s fits, for the encode
2 currents at 1 kHz 2 000 ~0.4 M any Cortex-M, trivially
4 properties at 4 kHz 16 000 ~3.4 M any Cortex-M, room to spare
5 properties at 25 kHz 125 000 ~27 M a fast M4F, an M7 easily

These tables count the encode, the Series writes of a batch. The whole poll_properties that sends it costs more: 75.5 k cycles for a 32-row FOC batch on the H725 at opt-level = 3 (a few properties, a batch with a current ramp, so more than the quiet one above), 13 % of the core at 25 kHz with the rows; 121.5 k at "z", 20 %. With the bit writer before SW-68 it was 99.9 k and 172.2 k (17 % and 27 %), and until API-52 191 k and 513 k (30 % and 77 %): whenever a batch's worst case didn't fit the outbox's free room, the poll measured it first by rebuilding each column of the batch into a scratch and walking it, and the outbox walked its entries for every lookup. In the profile of that poll on the Cortex-M4F model (74.8 k instructions; 104.8 k before SW-68, 207 k before API-52) the packed blocks and the delta code take 25.8 k (56.5 k before SW-68, when one put_bits per residual was 29.3 k alone), each column's framing and the scratch around it 11 k, memcpy 5.8 k; the rest is the update, its length and the outbox.

What carries it: USB full speed (about 1 MB/s in practice) carries none of them. Ethernet (the H725 has a 10/100 MAC, 12.5 MB/s line rate) carries every Series row of the table and the raw copy at 400 kHz. USB high speed (480 Mbit/s, 60 MB/s line rate; on the H725 it needs an external ULPI PHY) carries all of them, raw included.

What limits first:

  • Series: the encode in the event loop. The capture fits at every rate (a cells row is 12 to 53 % of a period; at 2 MHz half the period is a lot for an interrupt that also runs the control loop). The polls don't: 3 values at 400 kHz take 67 % of the core, which leaves little for the converter, and 1 MHz and 2 MHz need 1.6x and 2.9x the core. Per row the polls cost about 4.5 times the capture.
  • Raw: the link. 4.5 to 19 % of the CPU, but 6.4 to 24 MB/s: Ethernet up to 400 kHz, USB HS above.
  • The Series bytes are 2.9x to 3.1x fewer than raw, which is what moves 1 and 2 MHz from USB HS to Ethernet, if the CPU were there.

Where a poll's cycles go, from its profile on the Cortex-M4F model (18 400 instructions for a 3-value batch, 22 900 before the word-wide bit writer of SW-68): the codec itself (delta, packed blocks, bit writer, runs) about 5 100, 9 300 before; each column's own framing (count, tag runs, the check that no tag changes) 2 000, the ring copy 1 800, the update's framing, the outbox and the poll's own code the rest. API-52 took 3 to 6 % off these polls (the FOC poll lost half, see above): this bench's 4 KB outbox always had room for a batch's worst case, so its batches were never measured. SW-68 took 13 to 15 % off them on the H725 (17 % at "z"), and what is left is about as much codec as framing around it: less framing per column would move it next. So today a device observes a few values at every period up to a few hundred kHz, and above that decimates (every_us, a grid in whole µs) or sends raw over a fast link.

Flash for the encoder is 15.0 KB on a Cortex-M3 with DeltaPacked (9.8 KB on an M4F), 25.4 KB (17.6 KB) with a run-time CodecPolicy. A firmware no longer carries 64-bit numbers for the protocol's own types: a NumericValue takes 8 bytes instead of 16, and the H725 firmware got 7.7 KB smaller in flash. Every sample taken is encoded, also when its batch is later dropped for lack of budget, so an MCU sets a floor on the rate it accepts (ObserveLimits::samples_min_every_us): otherwise any host can ask for more encoding than the CPU has.

When not to batch¶

  • Latest value is enough. If the host wants to see a value, not every sample of it, the plain observe modes (periodic, on change) are smaller and simpler. Batch what you would plot or analyze.
  • One value at a time. A batch of 1 costs a byte more than plain; a quantity sent alone costs more than an f32. Batching pays from two items on and most of it by 16 to 32.
  • Latency matters more than bytes. A batch waits up to its max age before it goes; an event that must arrive now is a plain stream item or a method's answer.
  • Strings, byte lists, opaque fields. A Series<T> carries them, as runs, in a method's answer or a property. A batched stream or an observed +batched property doesn't: the batcher needs the worst case of an item to keep a batch inside its byte budget, and a string has no bound, so batched on such a type is a compile error. Observe cells need a fixed-size cell per value, which a string doesn't have either. A fixed-size array ([u8; 16]) has both, at a column per byte. Planned for text and frames: see Ahead.
  • The CPU can't keep up. Check the table above; decimate (every_us) or observe fewer properties.

Compared with raw globals¶

The usual way to get fast signals out of a controller is no protocol at all: copy the variables into a RAM buffer and send it as it is (a VESC-style sample buffer), or let the debugger read RAM while the core runs (TI-style RAM observe). How does a Series compare? Per FOC level batch (21 values and their time, 32 samples) on the STM32H725 at opt-level = 3, with 781 batches a second at 25 kHz:

one FOC level batch cycles bytes CPU at 25 kHz (of 540 MHz) link at 25 kHz
raw copy of the 22 arrays (memcpy) 2 800 2 816 0.4 % 2.2 MB/s
Series, column-wise DeltaPacked 36 800 1 251 5.3 % 0.98 MB/s
the whole poll_properties, cells 83 300 - about 14 % with the rows -

The link decides first. USB full speed carries about 1 MB/s in practice: the raw copy at full rate doesn't fit it, the Series just does. Over USB high speed or Ethernet both fit, and raw wins on CPU by about 13x for the encode, and by about 30x counting the whole poll that sends the batch (the third row, a 32-row batch of the FOC bench, which carries a current ramp).

What that CPU buys:

  • Meaning on the wire. Types, names, units and prefixes come with the device's introspection data, so ww, Python and a dynamic client show 4980 mA without a hand-kept list of offsets and scale factors.
  • True time and counted losses. Every sample carries its device time, and a sample that couldn't be sent is counted in missed. A buffer read by the debugger can tear a row or skip samples without telling anyone.
  • Mixed rates. Each trait level is observed at its own rate and budget, a 25 kHz tier next to a 1 kHz one.
  • Autoranging. A value may change its prefix from sample to sample, at almost no cost.
  • An API that evolves. Fields and properties can be added without breaking older hosts or devices.
  • Tooling. ww observe --samples, and numpy columns in Python with recv_batch().

What it costs besides CPU:

  • RAM: the level's two batch stores (or a ring and one staging batch with cells), 8.4 to 8.6 KB for the FOC level at 32 samples, and its outbox (observe_outbox, 4 KB by default): about 12.5 KB.
  • Flash: the encoder itself is 15.0 KB on a Cortex-M3 with DeltaPacked and 9.8 KB on a Cortex-M4F (the observe and batcher code around it is not counted there, and the H725 build wasn't measured for flash).

The capture gap is closed by observe cells: the interrupt writes each value into its cell with a plain store, as it would write a global, and commits the row: 342 cycles and 52 B of stack for the 21 FOC values at opt-level = 3. What stays is the event loop's side: the encode, 5.3 % above, and the poll around it, about 14 % in all. There, less framing per column is next (see Ahead), and aligned plain (measured, kept on file) was 34 200 cycles per batch for 2 882 bytes when it was measured, before SW-68: now hardly cheaper than column-wise DeltaPacked for 2.3x the bytes.

Nomicon

Nomicon: the bits and the cycles¶

How a Series is laid out bit by bit, what each codec does, and where the encoder's cycles went.

The column layout¶

A Series is a count, the columns, and padding to the next byte:

n:          UNib32 (nibble-aligned)     number of items; 0: nothing else follows
group(T):   the columns of T over all n items
padding:    zero bits to the next byte

The group of a type is written by its own rule, recursively: a struct is its fields' groups in declaration order (an evolvable one first writes its field count, or its bit length when nested, so an older reader can skip what it doesn't know); an enum is a tag run, then each variant's fields over only the items in that variant; Option is the same with two variants. Columns come depth first, which lets a reader find every column's start in one forward pass without a directory of offsets: the skim validates every code, records a cursor per column, and iter() then steps all columns in parallel, zero-copy, without allocating.

Predictors¶

A numeric column header is 4 bits: 2 for the predictor, 2 for the coder.

  • raw: every value as it is (zigzag for signed types).
  • delta: the segment's first value in full, then the zigzag of each difference to the previous value.
  • delta of delta: the zigzag of the change of that difference. A regular clock becomes all zeros, a ramp after a step too; on a flat noisy signal it amplifies the noise (a second difference), so it's no universal win.

A segment starts at the first item and at every restart; the predictor state starts over there. The fourth predictor code, 11, is reserved as an escape for later column modes, so a new mode never changes what an existing code means.

Coders¶

  • prefix: each residual in the variable-length delta code (0 for zero, 10 + 7 bits, 110 + 9, ...).
  • packed: blocks of 8 residuals, a width field (5 bits for 16-bit values, 6 for 32), then each residual at exactly that width. A block of zeros costs only its width field.
  • constant: the first residual once; every later one equals it. Delta + constant is t0 + period.
  • plain: every residual at full width.

Packing is where most of the gain over the older prefix code came from: a ±1 LSB delta costs 9 bits as a prefix code and 2 to 3 packed. Over 100 values, a steady voltage column is 659 bits with the prefix code and 359 packed. The prefix code still wins on flat data with rare jumps (235 against 433 bits), because a single jump widens a whole block of 8, which is why the writer can choose per column, and why columns restart at a range switch: 110 B with restarts against 118 B without on the range-switch signal.

XOR floats¶

A float column is plain, or the XOR code known from Gorilla: each value XORed with the previous one, then only the meaningful bits between the leading and trailing zeros, reusing the previous window when it fits. A repeated value is one bit. It does what it can, but a noisy float changes most of its mantissa every sample: on the motor demo, batching f32 values only went from 4.57 to 3.42 bytes per value, while the same values as I32 with a prefix went to 1.74. Integers with a prefix were about 60 % of the whole gain. The showcase walks through XOR-coded temperatures bit by bit.

How the encoder got 6x faster and 6x smaller¶

The first encoder was correct and slow. Profiled under QEMU, a 32-item batch with fixed(delta, packed) took 63 359 instructions on a Cortex-M3, and they went to:

  • 27 %: restart checks building the tag arrays of every field, per item per column, also for fields that can't have a tag;
  • 24 %: the packed coder's look-ahead, which ran the predictor twice per item;
  • 20 %: nested walks over the items of each field;
  • 15 %: the bit writer, a read-modify-write per byte with 64-bit masks.

Flash had its own story: 16 copies of a ~4 KB column writer, one per leaf position and sink, each with all codecs and the trials. The fixes, each measured on its own: packed blocks buffered instead of looked ahead; restarts compare only fields that can carry a tag; enums write only the variants their tag run saw; the predictor works on left-aligned values in a u32 for leaves up to 32 bits; SMALLEST sizes the four coders of a predictor in one pass; the bit writer keeps the open byte in a register; column writers are small structs stepped per item, one copy per word size; and policies became types (FixedCodecs), so a firmware links only its codecs. The bytes are the same as before, down to the golden vectors.

Why the shared clock halves the CPU¶

A Timed<T> item has a time column next to its value, and a time is itself a quantity: a prefix run, a storage tag run and a value column, the same three columns as the value. So the time is half of the columns an encoder walks per item, and sharing one time column across a level's properties removes nearly half the work. On the H725, one foc level batch (21 properties, 32 samples):

Per sample that is 786 → 379 cycles, and the full 25 kHz FOC rate went from about 77 % of the H725 to 37 %. (Both measured in one build at opt-level = "z"; the same bench rebuilt later took 302 k cycles for the shared form with QEMU's instruction count unchanged: at "z" the M7's timing moves with the code layout, the work doesn't.) The bytes drop more than expected because DeltaPacked has no constant coder: each 40 µs step is a 7-bit residual, so each property's time column cost about 36 B per batch, 38 % of the update. One shared column is 37 B for the whole level. QEMU agrees: 46 % fewer instructions on both the M3 and the M4F.

Straight from the store¶

Which part of that is the codec and which is walking items? Two experiments on the same FOC level batch answer it.

The first is a column-wise writer: instead of rebuilding each item from the batcher's struct-of-arrays store and walking the items once per column, it reads each column's array straight from the store, one pass per column. It writes the same bytes as the item walk (a property test over every policy checks it) whenever no enum or Option tag changes inside the batch; a batch with such a change, strings, or the odd-width UN/IN numbers still takes the walk. The second is aligned plain: values copied byte-aligned in their own width, no prediction, no bit packing, behind the predictor escape. Below both, an upper bound: the 22 payload arrays of the batch copied with memcpy.

The surprise is the third row: walking items with aligned columns is already 44 k cycles at opt-level = 3. So the item walk was not the main cost; the bit writer and the codec are. Column-wise, a column still costs about 3.3 k instructions on a Cortex-M3: about 1.2 k for the delta and block code, 1.2 k for writing bits (24 bits per push, one byte per loop), and 0.8 k for the column's own Series framing (count, two tag runs, the checks). The bit writer part is what SW-68 took down: a column is 1 601 instructions on the Cortex-M4F model now, against 2 510 before.

Aligned plain was measured and is not recommended. From the store it was 1.75x cheaper than column-wise DeltaPacked at opt-level = 3 (34 k against 60 k cycles; since SW-68 DeltaPacked is 37 k), but slower at "z" (129 k against 100 k; 53 k now), and it sends 2.3x the bytes: 2.25 MB/s for the FOC level at 25 kHz, more than a full-speed USB link carries. It wasn't a plain copy either: when it was measured, the store kept every NumericValue payload as a u64 (firmware carried 64-bit numbers for the protocol's own types, since dropped), so an I32 column was narrowed value by value, and the framing of 22 separate series, their tag runs and checks, already costs 5x the memcpy bound. And a new wire form is one that every older reader rejects. It stays an option for a device on USB HS or Ethernet that needs the last bit of CPU. The column-wise writer, in contrast, changes no byte on the wire: it is purely a faster way to write the same Series, and it is what the batcher uses.

Observe cells: the interrupt side¶

The cells were measured against sample() on a no_std copy of the motor demo's 21 FOC properties: 64 real FOC ticks, a host observing all 21 in Samples mode, poll_properties every 16 rows. Three details from the measurement:

  • Inlining decides "z". With plain #[inline] the setters called the cell writes out of line, and cells were no faster than sample() (2 055 against 2 022 cycles). The setters and the writes are #[inline(always)] now. sample()'s own copy into the store has the same problem at "z" (1 122 instructions against 260 at 3).
  • Nobody observing costs cells more. sample() returns before copying when no host wants the row; the setters store anyway: 310 against 180 cycles at "z", 303 against 189 at 3. Wrap them, or the expensive values, in if w.is_sampling() { .. }.
  • The work moves, it doesn't vanish. A poll copies the waiting rows from the ring, about 440 cycles a row at 3 with all 21 observed (10.1 k against 3.1 k cycles for 16 rows; only the observed columns are copied, 4.4 k for 3 of them), and the poll that encodes costs about 7 % more.

And what 64-bit payloads cost before they were dropped from firmware: the same row took 445 against 435 cycles at 3 and the level's samples 14.3 KB of RAM either way, against 8.6 KB with 32-bit payloads.

What a struct series costs: the restart check¶

The obvious alternative, the level batch as one Series<Timed<LevelRow>> with an Option<T> field per property, was measured and dropped. It is a little smaller (1 233 B against 1 251 B) but took about 2.0 M cycles on the H725 against 0.3 M. Inside a struct, a column restarts its predictor when a tag of an earlier field changes. With 21 tagged quantity fields that check is quadratic per item, and a decimated property's None rows would restart every later column's deltas. Restarts are the right rule for a quantity's own prefix; across unrelated properties they're pure cost, so a level is separate series per column.

The bit writer and reader, a word at a time¶

After the column-wise writer, the bits themselves were the biggest cost left: in the FOC poll's profile on the Cortex-M4F model (104.8 k instructions), put_bits alone was 29.3 k and the packed blocks around it 27.2 k. SW-68 rewrote shrink_wrap's writers and readers to move a word at a time, with the same bytes (every golden vector, the conformance corpus and the fuzz round trips unchanged, no unsafe):

  • BitWriter (the Series sink) keeps a 32-bit accumulator in registers and stores four bytes when it fills, instead of a byte; the room left is cached, so a push is one compare, a shift and an or.
  • BitSink::put_block writes a packed block (its width field and all residuals) in one call: one bounds check for the block, the accumulator in locals for the loop. delta_packed_slice and the packed coder use it.
  • BufWriter::write_un*, write_bool (requests, replies, the non-Series paths) do one read-modify-write of a 4-byte big-endian window instead of a loop per byte, with one bounds check per call; only the last 3 bytes of a buffer take a cold byte-wise path.
  • UNib32 writes its nibbles as one run of bits, and BufReader::take_unib32 reads one in a single loop.
  • BufReader::read_un32 / read_un64 read through the same 4-byte window, one bounds check, and a failing read moves nothing.

  • More from cells. The snapshot modes (periodic, on change) could read a cells level's latest row instead of calling getters, and batched streams fed from an interrupt (ADC samples) could get the same cells.
  • Strings and frames in batches. Codecs across items (API-51, a "same" bit per string, a frame XOR'd with the last one) and dictionary compression for strings (SW-28) would make text cheaper per item; frame splitters for byte streams (STD-41: newline, idle gap, length field, CRC frames, COBS, Modbus RTU) would cut a UART or CAN byte stream into bounded frames, which a batched stream can take.
  • Less framing per column. With the poll's own bookkeeping gone (API-52), what is left around the codec is each column's framing: its count, its tag runs and the check that no tag changes, about 500 instructions a column on a Cortex-M4F; one framing for a whole level would make the high rates cheaper.
  • Room kept in the format. The reserved predictor escape is for a common factor per column, the values divided by it before the predictor, as ALP chooses its scale per block; the float codes have room for better float coders (Chimp, ALP). Diffs across batches would save the first values, about 8 B per batch, but only on reliable streams and with resync rules after a gap; not worth it at 32 items.

The feature tracker has the state of all of this: SW-60, SW-62, SW-63, SW-64, SW-68 for the type, the store, the fast encoder, the column-wise writer and the word-wide bit writer, API-41, API-39, API-49 and API-50 for batched streams, batched observe, the shared clock and observe cells.
