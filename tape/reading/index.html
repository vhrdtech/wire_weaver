Reading a tape¶

A reader of a tape usually wants a little of it: one track out of thousands, one hour out of a week, an overview of a signal. This page explains how TapeReader gets there with few reads, how it finds the definitions and state in force at a place, and what it does with damage. It assumes Tape concepts.

Byte sources¶

TapeReader reads through a byte source (ByteSource): anything that has a length and reads bytes at an offset. A byte slice, a file and the bytes of a memory sink being written (MemSink::tape) are byte sources, and an HTTP resource read by range requests can be one. The reader needs no file system and no threads, so it also builds for WebAssembly and runs in a browser: it needs the crate's alloc feature, not std. The block walk FileBlocks of the last section needs std.

Opening from the tails¶

TapeReader::open(source) finds the pieces from the end of the bytes, because a finished piece describes itself there:

  1. It reads the last 164 bytes. A good tail gives the last piece's length, so its start, and where its finish block is.
  2. Just before that start lies the previous piece's tail, if there is one. The reader steps back piece by piece with a few small reads each: the tail, the finish and the piece head.
  3. A piece without a tail, one being written or cut off by a crash, is found from its newest checkpoint. The reader looks backwards from the end for an index block, at most OpenOptions::scan_back_max (256 MiB by default). It follows the chain of checkpoints back to list the blocks before it, and scans the blocks after it one by one.
  4. A piece without any checkpoint, one shorter than about 4 MiB, is scanned from its start, or from the end of the finished piece before it.

TapeReader::pieces then lists each piece as a PieceInfo: where it starts and ends, its head, finish and tail when it has them, and how it was found (Found::Tail, Checkpoint or Scan, with the bytes scanned). A scan stops at the first block that isn't good, and PieceInfo::cut names that offset. PieceInfo::gaps lists blank or bad stretches the scan read past, and PieceInfo::replaced lists blocks that were written twice. A piece of another format version has PieceInfo::version set; every read of it is refused with ReadError::Version.

A raw medium, such as an SD card, isn't read from its end, where an older recording's tail may pass for the newest piece. Open it with TapeReader::open_with and OpenOptions::medium set to Medium::Raw: the reader then walks forward from the medium's start, or from a hint its medium layer gives. Damage and recovery explains why.

Finding blocks through the index¶

A reader finds blocks through the piece's index, never by walking the whole file:

  • TapeReader::entries(i) gives every block of piece i, in file order, as IndexEntrys: offset, length, kind, track, first item number, item count, first and last tick, and the latest end of any item.
  • TapeReader::track_entries(i, track_id) gives one track's blocks. In a finished piece it reads only the final index's directory and that track's list, each checked by its own CRC, so a reader of one track out of thousands reads a few hundred bytes of index. Track id 0 gives the record blocks of data, and STATE_TRACK_ID the state entries.
  • TapeReader::read_block(i, entry, buf) reads the block an entry names and checks it.
  • TapeReader::read_chunk_head(i, entry, buf) reads only the head of the chunk an entry names, up to its payload, and checks it by the head CRC: what a chunk is, and a series chunk's grid and scale, without its samples.
  • TapeReader::series_timeline(i, track_id, period) reads the head of every chunk of a series track and gives its SeriesTimeline, beside the errors of the heads it left out (Events and series).
  • TapeReader::state_entries(i) gives piece i's state entries, each with its index entry.

An entry tells a reader whether it needs a block before reading it. A query for the items between two ticks takes the entries whose tick range overlaps; a query for item 1 000 000 takes the entry whose item range holds it.

State at a place¶

Before a reader can decode items it needs the definitions in force where the items are: what each track is, which clock it counts, what type its items have. A track that holds state needs its item in force too. The format has no "current state" of a whole file: what is in force depends on the place. The reader looks it up through the index, without reading the blocks in between.

Every state record has its own index entry, with its slot and the hash of its item. Three lookups use them:

  • TapeReader::state_at(i, slot, offset) gives the record of one slot in force at offset in piece i: the newest one whose block starts before the place.
  • TapeReader::definitions_at(i, offset) gives every definition in force at a place, by slot (DefinitionsAt). Pass u64::MAX for the end of the piece.
  • TapeReader::item_by_hash(i, hash) gives the newest record whose item has that hash, of any slot. An application that refers to a state item by its hash finds it this way.

The writer keeps every state item near its use, so the record a lookup finds usually lies within near (32 MiB by default) behind the place. For example, a viewer that opens a week-long piece at 15:00 on Wednesday looks up the offset of that hour in the index, asks definitions_at for it, and gets the definitions it needs from a few small reads, however much lies before.

Damage is a reason to look further, never an error. A lookup whose newest record doesn't read lists it as lost and takes the next older record of the slot. It also names the stretches the index doesn't cover (uncovered), such as a gap a scan read past or the bytes past a cut, since a newer record may lie there: for state_at those between the record and the place, for definitions_at those before the place, and for item_by_hash those after the record. A lookup reads at most OpenOptions::state_read_max bytes (1 GiB by default). When that budget runs out, the lookup stops and sets spent: the answer is incomplete, but nothing in the file is damaged. is_whole says that nothing was lost, uncovered or left unread.

Reading items¶

How a reader gets at the items depends on the block:

  • A record block. Block::records walks its records in order: track, time and item bytes. A body coded with lz4 (header flag FLAG_BODY_CODED) is decoded first with Block::decode, whose result has the same records.
  • An events chunk. Block::chunk gives the chunk's head and columns. EventTimes::read reads its times in place: starts().get(j) gives item j's start tick, lower_bound(t) the first item at or after tick t, last_at_or_before(t) the last item at or before it, and end(j) and flag(j) its end and flags. Payloads::read gives each item's bytes with get(j), without decoding the items before it: in place for a raw payload column, and from one decode of the whole column for an lz4 one.
  • A series chunk. Samples::of takes the samples column, and Samples::decode decodes any range of samples from the group that holds its first sample. The chunk's grid gives each sample's tick and its scale; SeriesTimeline collects the grids of a track's chunks, checks each chunk against its neighbours and says where the gaps are.
  • A summary block. It holds coarse statistics of a long track, for an overview without reading the items. Events and series describes them.

Item j of a chunk is item first item number + j of its track. A reader so goes from an item number to the chunk through the index, and from the chunk to the item by its position. Pieces and files explains why items are addressed this way.

Checking a piece¶

TapeReader::verify(i) hashes piece i's bytes before its finish and compares the result with the content hash the finish states. A match proves the piece unchanged since it was written. TapeReader::chain checks the links between the pieces at hand: each piece head against the piece before it, and each tail's next id against the next piece.

A piece without a finish has no hash to check. That is how a reader knows the piece wasn't closed.

Damage and salvage¶

Every block carries a CRC-32C, and a reader checks it before using the block. Most damage therefore costs one block, and the index still says what that block held: which track, which items, which ticks.

Inside a chunk the reader can save more:

  • A chunk's head has a CRC of its own, and its payload a CRC per 64 KiB. With a good head and a damaged payload, Block::salvage hands out the chunk with its bad segments marked (Block::is_intact false, Chunk::bad_segments, Chunk::range_damaged).
  • EventTimes::salvage and Payloads::salvage check an events chunk's columns group by group, each group of 128 items against its neighbours. Only the items of a group that fails are marked suspect (is_suspect), and of a raw payload column also the items with a byte in a bad segment; the rest read as usual. A damaged lz4 payload column makes every item suspect.
  • FileBlocks with Recovery::Resync walks a damaged file block by block and finds the next good block after a bad stretch, where the default Recovery::Prefix stops.

Damage and recovery gives the full picture: what each kind of damage costs and what the format can't protect against.
