Damage and recovery¶

Recordings fail in ordinary ways. Power goes out in the middle of a write, a cable is pulled, an SD card is reused without being erased, a sector goes bad on an old disk. A tape is built so that each of these costs the bytes it touched and nothing else, and so that a reader says what was lost instead of guessing. This page explains how, then closes with what the format does not protect against. It assumes the overview and refers to chunks from Events and series.

Checksums at every level¶

Damage has to be found before it can be contained. A tape checks its bytes at four levels:

Check Covers Damage costs
block CRC-32C each whole block that block
head CRC-32C the head of a chunk or index block the head alone; the block's place stays known
CRC segment, CRC-32C each 64 KiB of a chunk's payload one 64 KiB stretch of one chunk
content hash, BLAKE3 every byte of a finished piece before its finish block nothing; it proves the piece is unchanged

The head CRC matters most. A chunk's head gives its length, its track, its item numbers and ticks, and the CRC of each 64 KiB stretch of its payload. When the payload is damaged but the head is good, a reader still trusts the length (so it finds the next block) and still knows exactly which items were hit. A flipped bit in a 4 MiB series chunk costs one 64 KiB CRC segment of samples.

Every CRC is CRC-32C. A writer and a reader compute it with the CPU's CRC instructions where it has them (SSE4.2 on x86-64, the CRC extension of ARMv8) and with a table elsewhere, such as in a browser or on a microcontroller; the result is the same, so the choice never shows in the file.

The checks detect damage; they do not correct it. Correcting is left to the medium: a disk's or flash controller's error correction, or a file system. A block kind is reserved for parity blocks, but no writer writes them.

Reading past damage¶

FileBlocks walks the blocks of a byte stream in order, in one of two modes:

  • Prefix (the default) takes the longest valid prefix. It stops at the first block with a bad magic, length or CRC, at a block cut short, at a sequence number that goes backwards, and at a good block of another session where no session may start (stale data), and says where it stopped. A blank or bad stretch within the layout's longest gap is reported as a gap and read past (below). This is what a crash leaves: good blocks, then a torn one.
  • Resync reads on. It reports a bad block and looks for the next block magic one byte after the bad block's start. Any byte may start a block, and a block is taken only when its CRCs match. A chunk with a good head and a damaged payload is handed out with its bad CRC segments marked, and the walk goes on after it by its length. In a stretch that Prefix reads past as a gap, Resync hands out the good blocks too, also a session that starts there and the blocks after it. Past the gap, the walk goes on with the session it had, where Prefix does. So Resync reads every block Prefix reads.

Inside a damaged events chunk, a reader can still salvage items. The time codec and the payload lengths are stored per group of 128 items, and each group's record must agree with its neighbours: offsets that chain, ticks in order, widths in range. A group that breaks the chain is marked suspect, and only its items are. A flipped bit in one residual damages one item; in a group record, one group or two. A payload column coded with lz4 is the exception: one bad byte makes every item of the chunk suspect, since the column decodes as a whole.

Writing so a crash costs little¶

TapeWriter writes a file the way databases write their logs:

  1. It creates the file as NAME.partial and syncs the file and its directory, so the name can't vanish in a crash.
  2. It syncs the data by a policy: never, every N blocks, or at most an interval apart (1 s by default). What a crash can lose is bounded by the policy.
  3. At the finish it writes the final index, the finish block (padded so the piece ends on 4 KiB) and the tail, then renames the file to NAME and syncs the directory again.

A failed write or sync ends the file. The writer reports it and refuses every later write; it never retries, because after a failed fsync Linux may drop the unwritten pages, and a second fsync then succeeds without them. A file that still ends in .partial was never finished.

Resuming after a crash¶

TapeWriter::resume reopens NAME.partial and continues the same piece. It reads the piece the way TapeReader does, from the newest checkpoint and then by a scan that stops at the first bad block. It keeps every good block up to the end of the piece's data, and cuts off the rest: a torn block, an end sequence, and puts marked FLAG_WITH_NEXT that end what it keeps (they belong with a block that didn't survive). The resume replays every block it keeps, so the writer knows again what it put and where. A damaged block the index still lists counts as a newer record of each slot it held, item unknown, so the next put of such a slot writes. The producer passes the same writer options it wrote the piece with (TapeWriter::options gives them) and gives again every call from the block next_seq reports, with the same definitions: a resumed piece then ends with the same bytes, and the same content hash, as one written without the crash. A piece found with its finish (a crash after the finish, before the rename) is opened again the same way: its finish and tail are cut off, and the next finish pads the piece to 4 KiB again.

The resume checks those options against the piece before it cuts or writes anything. The newest layout and producer the piece kept must be the ones the options give (WriteError::Layout, WriteError::Producer, each naming both). Options with another chunk alignment would place blocks the way the file says it doesn't, and a reader trusts the layout when it looks for the end of the data. The layout's gap settings need no check, because the writer never leaves a gap.

The writer holds no definitions of its own, so a resume needs none from the file: the application passes its definitions with each call, as before the crash, and the writer puts what each block needs again where it doesn't lie near behind.

For example, a recorder loses power while writing piece 3 of a chain. On restart it calls PieceChain::resume with the directory and the chain options. The chain reads pieces 0 to 2 back from their tails, reopens 00000003.wwt.partial, and the recorder goes on from the first block the file lacks. A crash between finishing one piece and creating the next resumes too: the chain creates the next piece.

A writer writes through a sink: a file (FileSink) or memory (MemSink). A memory sink reads back as a byte source, so TapeReader reads a piece while it is being written, and tests drive crashes by cutting the bytes anywhere. PieceChain takes a factory that makes the sink for each piece, Files or MemTapes.

Example: a recorder that lost power¶

A recorder on a PC writes a tape of several tracks, 50 MiB so far, with the default sync interval of 1 s. The power fails. On disk is capture.wwt.partial: every block up to the last sync, and perhaps part of the block being written.

A reader opens it. The file has no tail, so the reader looks backwards from the end for the newest checkpoint index, which is at most about 4 MiB back. From that checkpoint it follows the chain of checkpoints back and so lists every block before it, without reading them. It then scans the blocks after the newest checkpoint, one by one, and stops at the torn block, which fails its CRC. The reader reports the piece as unfinished, cut at that offset.

The recording loses the torn block and whatever the writer had not yet synced: at most about one second of data. Everything else reads, including the definitions and the summary blocks written so far. The content hash can't be checked, because the finish block was never written; that is how a reader knows the file was not closed.

Sessions and stale data¶

On a file, the end of the data is the end of the file. On a raw medium, such as an SD card or raw NAND flash written without a file system, it is not: the card may hold an older, longer recording behind the current one, and that recording's blocks are as valid as the new ones.

A session is one writer's unbroken append: a producer start, a device boot. Every block of a session carries the same 32-bit session tag in its header, and its sequence numbers count from 0. A writer picks the tag at random, or derives it from the piece id when the same input must give the same bytes; never 0 nor FFFFFFFFh, so a blank header never names a session. A reader keeps to one session at a time. A good block of another session is stale, unless a new session may start there.

Where a new session may start depends on the medium:

  • In a file, any record block that starts with a definition (another producer appended its blocks) or any piece head (files concatenated) may start a session.
  • On a raw medium, a new session starts only at a piece head that names the session just ended as its previous session. Stale data from an earlier recording names some other session and is never taken, even when it starts with a piece head of its own.

A block is judged by its session before its format version. Stale data written by an older firmware in another format is therefore the end of the data, like any other stale data, not a piece of that format.

A raw medium is therefore read forward, from its start or from a hint the medium layer gives, never from its end, where a stale tail could pass for the newest piece.

A finished piece is a multiple of 4 KiB long on a raw medium too, so a piece that starts on a 4 KiB boundary ends on one. The medium's layout record is unchanged by it: gap_max and resume_align say where a writer goes on after a blank or bad stretch, and a piece appended after a finished one starts on a resume place whenever resume_align divides 4 KiB (a 512-byte sector).

Blank space and gaps¶

Blank bytes are all 0x00 or all 0xFF: what an erased or preallocated medium reads, depending on its kind. No block starts with either, so a blank stretch running to the end of the file or medium is the end of the data.

A blank or damaged stretch in the middle is not always the end. A NAND writer whose page program failed moves on to the next block; a writer padding a NAND page at a sync leaves 0xFF behind. The definitions' layout says how far a writer may skip and where it resumes:

Medium Longest gap Resumes at a multiple of
file 0: the first bad stretch is the end 1 byte
SD card or eMMC 4 MiB 512 bytes, a sector
raw NAND two erase blocks one page

Past a blank or bad stretch, a reader reads one block header at the first non-blank byte and at each resume place within the longest gap. A good block of the current session that goes on with the data there makes the stretch a gap: reported with its length, and the walk goes on. When there is none, the stretch is the end of the data.

The format bounds what a reader pays per gap. A valid layout allows at most 64 MiB of gap with at most 8192 resume places, and a reader checks at most 4 whole blocks per gap. On a raw medium, blank space longer than the gap is the end, and nothing after it is read: an erased 64 GB card costs one gap's worth of header reads. A Resync walk looks once per stretch too, and only up to where Prefix ends; it reads up to one gap and a block ahead, 128 MiB at most.

Duplicates¶

A writer that retried a write may leave the same block twice. The same session and sequence number twice is a duplicate, not a cut. With the same bytes, the second copy is dropped. With other bytes, the later copy is taken and the replacement is reported.

Example: a logger card recorded twice¶

A data logger records to an SD card as a raw medium. Its first recording, session A, filled 100 MiB. The card is reused: session B writes 30 MiB from the start, then the power fails during a write.

A reader walks forward from the start of the card. It takes B's blocks up to the torn one, then probes for B's next block within 4 MiB. It finds only A's old blocks, which carry A's session tag and don't start with a piece head naming B. They are stale, so the data ends at the torn block, and none of A's 70 remaining MiB is mistaken for B's.

When the logger boots again, it finds the same end of data and writes its next session after it, starting with a piece head that names B as the previous session. That session, C, is taken by the next reader; A's leftovers beyond C stay stale.

What a damaged file still gives¶

The three cases below cover where damage lands in a file. "Lost" means unreadable: overwritten, zeroed or bad sectors.

The first 4 KiB are lost. These hold the piece head, the first definitions and perhaps the first data blocks.

  • A finished file opens from its tail as usual. The final index lists every block, and every block outside the lost range reads. Each definition comes from its newest record that reads: a copy at a use 1 MiB after its first write, or a put of it near a later block of its data. The records and chunks inside the lost range are lost, and the content hash no longer matches. The link back to the previous piece in a chain can't be checked, since the head named it; the link forward still can.
  • An unfinished file with at least one checkpoint opens from its newest checkpoint, and the same holds.
  • An unfinished file shorter than one checkpoint interval (about 4 MiB) is scanned from its start, and the scan stops at the damage: TapeReader lists nothing after it. A walk in Resync mode reads every good block after the damage.

The last 4 KiB are lost. These hold the tail and the finish, and often part of the final index. The reader finds no tail and reads the file as unfinished: it finds the newest checkpoint and scans up to the damage. Every data block before the damage reads. The content hash and the link forward to the next piece are lost, and the file reports as unfinished instead of complete.

One block in the middle is lost. The cost depends on its kind:

Lost block Cost
events chunk, payload damaged the items of the groups it hits are marked suspect; the rest read
events chunk, head damaged the chunk's items; the index still says which track, items and ticks they were
series chunk one 64 KiB CRC segment per hit with a good head; the whole chunk with a bad head
record block its records; the index entry says how many, the gap in sequence numbers how many blocks
summary nothing; it is rebuilt from the data
definitions or puts nothing once a copy or a later put of each of its items reads; until then, what it held
checkpoint index in an unfinished piece, the piece up to the next good checkpoint is scanned instead
final index in a finished piece, the lists of blocks: a read of every entry fails, while a track's own list still reads when the index head and that list are good

In a finished file a reader reads blocks by the final index, so a lost block in the middle other than the final index costs that block alone. In an unfinished file the same holds before the newest checkpoint. After it, the reader's scan stops at the lost block, and a Resync walk reads what follows.

What tape and flash media taught the format¶

Tape drives and flash memory solved these problems long before; the format takes their answers where they fit. Each point below names its source.

  • A tag per write session. LTO marks every data set with a write pass number that grows whenever data is overwritten, so a reader tells current data from residue (ECMA-319, §13.5.16). UBI stamps an image sequence number on every eraseblock for the same reason (Linux ubi-media.h). The session tag does this job. It is random instead of a counter, because a wiped card or a device without stored state has no counter to continue.
  • Nothing after the end is data. LTO declares data recorded after the end-of-data set inaccessible and invalid (ECMA-319, §13.4.3). The format has no end-of-data block, since a crash leaves none; blank space and the session tag mark the end instead.
  • Erased is not always zero. NAND erases to 0xFF (Micron TN-29-19). An SD card reads 0 or 1 after an erase, by vendor (SD Physical Layer Simplified Specification 9.10, §4.3.5.1), and eMMC reports which (JESD84-A441). So blank is 0x00 or 0xFF.
  • A blank unit isn't the end. A NAND writer that hits a failed program moves on and leaves erased pages behind; UBIFS looks past such units when it scans for the end of written data (Linux fs/ubifs/scan.c, recovery.c). The layout's gap and resume alignment say how far to look. Sizes follow the media: SD allocation units, up to 64 MB (SD 9.10, §4.13.1), and NAND erase blocks and pages (ONFI 6.0, §3.1).
  • Program a NAND page once. Programming the same part of a page twice without an erase gives undefined contents (ONFI 6.0, §6.7.1.25), and Micron's MLC example allows one program per page (Micron TN-29-19). A writer on raw NAND pads the rest of the page at each sync, and readers take the padding as a gap.
  • Rewrites: the later copy wins. LTO rewrites an interrupted data set in its entirety, and the later write counts (ECMA-319, §15.4). The same block twice in a tape is a duplicate, and the later copy is taken.
  • Indexes that point at themselves and back. An LTFS index carries a self pointer and a pointer to the index before it, and an index whose self pointer doesn't match is taken as data (LTFS Format Specification 2.5.1, §5.4.2, §5.4.3). A tape's index blocks carry the same two offsets, and a reader checks the self offset against where it found the block.
  • Never grow incompressible data. SLDC switches to a scheme that copies bytes, so incompressible data grows by about 0.05 % instead of 12.5 % (ECMA-321, §7.2). A writer that codes a block's body keeps the plain body when coding doesn't shrink it.
  • Correction belongs to the medium. ONFI leaves error correction to the host controller, per page (ONFI 6.0, §6.7.1.26). A tape only detects what that correction missed.

Limits¶

The format does not do everything. These limits follow from the design:

  • No correction. Checksums detect damage; they don't repair it. The parity block kind is reserved, and nothing writes or reads it.
  • Unfinished small files. A file without a checkpoint yet, under about 4 MiB, is read by a scan from its start, which stops at the first bad block. Only a Resync walk reads past it.
  • Definitions near the start. A definition's copy is written at a use at least 1 MiB after its first write. An unfinished file shorter than that holds each definition once, so one bad sector can take it. Chunks then still place themselves by track id, item number and tick, but without clock rates and item types a reader can't turn ticks into seconds or bytes into values.
  • Lost state in a scanned stretch. The lookups (TapeReader::state_at, definitions_at) go by index entries. A damaged block where a scan stopped has no known kind and no entries; the piece reports it as its cut, the lookups take the newest record before it and name the stretch past the cut as one the index doesn't cover.
  • A damaged final index. A finished piece whose final index doesn't read lists no blocks through TapeReader::entries; track_entries still gives each track whose own list reads. The checkpoints of the piece are not used in its place.
  • A damaged chunk head costs the chunk. The head CRC protects the payload's CRC segments, not itself.
  • lz4 payload columns. One bad byte makes every item of the chunk suspect.
  • 32-bit session tags. Stale data matches the current session by chance with probability 2⁻³² per stale session.
  • MLC paired pages. On MLC NAND, a power cut can corrupt an earlier, already synced page paired with the one being programmed. Keeping unsynced writes away from synced pages is the medium layer's task, not the format's.
  • The content hash needs a finished piece. An unfinished or cut-off piece has no hash to check.
  • Bursty nanosecond times cost more than varints. Random access costs about 1.4 bytes per event on such data (What it costs).
  • Fine ticks need epochs. A 64-bit tick at 1 ps spans about 106 days; longer captures are cut into clock epochs.
  • Reserved, not built. A payload column holding one shrink_wrap Series<T>, run-coded discrete samples, struct samples with a column per field, outer codecs on chunk columns, and preallocated space. Today's readers refuse the reserved numbers, so files that use them later won't be misread.
