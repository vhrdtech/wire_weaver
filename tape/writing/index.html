Writing a tape¶

This page follows a producer from an empty file to a finished piece, and through a crash and back. It explains what TapeWriter does for the producer and what the producer must do itself. It assumes the words of Tape concepts.

The writer and its sink¶

TapeWriter appends the producer's blocks and adds the blocks a piece needs around them: the definitions and state each block needs, checkpoints, the final index, the finish and the tail. The producer makes the data; the writer places it.

The writer puts its bytes into a sink. Two sinks come with ww_tape:

  • FileSink, the default, writes a file. TapeWriter::create(path, sync) creates it as NAME.partial and renames it to NAME at the finish. It refuses a NAME that is there (WriteError::Exists) and writes over a NAME.partial left behind; reopen such a piece with TapeWriter::resume to keep it. TapeWriter::append(path, sync) appends to a file in a new session, and creates the file when it isn't there.
  • MemSink keeps the bytes in memory, where a TapeReader reads them while they are written. Tests use it to cut a piece at any byte and check what a reader makes of it.

A chain of pieces takes a sink factory instead, which makes the sink of each piece: Files makes files in a directory, and MemTapes keeps every piece in memory under its name. Any other place, such as a raw SD card or a network store, needs its own TapeSink: a sink appends, syncs, reports its size and its path, gives the piece its final name at the finish, and for a resume reads back at an offset and truncates.

A MemSink of a chain reserves the room of the chain's cut size (CutPolicy::bytes plus CUT_SLACK) when its piece starts, so a reader's read_at never waits for the piece to be copied into a bigger buffer. MemTapes::remove(path) takes a finished piece out of the map: a reader that holds its MemTape reads on, the piece still being written is refused (WriteError::Unfinished), and what remains of the chain reads as a head cut, each piece giving its definitions and newest state from its own head. A chain with removed pieces is not resumed.

The writer's settings are its WriterOptions: the sync policy, the index policy, the layout (how blocks are placed), the producer's name and the near policy. The writer puts the layout and the producer at the start of every piece. They are the writer's own, and a caller can't write them.

What the application does with its definitions¶

The writer keeps no table of definitions. The application keeps the definitions of what it works on in a Definitions container and passes it, by reference, with every call that writes data. It does three things:

  1. It holds each clock, clock map, bundle and track it uses with Definitions::define, or several at once with define_all. The container checks each definition against those it holds: a track must name a clock held, and a clock epoch must come with its link to the epoch before. Nothing is written yet.
  2. It passes the container with every write of data (write_block, write_chunk, defer_block) and every put. Data on a track the container doesn't hold is refused.
  3. It drops a track it stops working on with Definitions::close. The clocks, maps and bundles no held track names go with it. Nothing is written for that either.

The writer does the rest. Before every block of data it puts the definitions the block's tracks need: the clocks, maps and bundles a track names, then the track itself. Each put writes only where the same definition doesn't lie near behind, so a block usually costs no definitions at all.

let mut defs = Definitions::new();
defs.define_all(&[clock, temperature_track, mode_track])?;
writer.put(&defs, MODE, ticks, &mode_bytes)?; // a state item, at its use
writer.write_block(&defs, &readings)?; // the definitions it needs go first

Here clock and the two tracks are Describe values (a ClockDef and two TrackDefs), and readings is a finished records block from a BlockWriter (see below). The repository runs these lines as a test (ww_tape/tests/it/book.rs).

How many definitions to hold is the application's choice; Definitions::len and Definitions::bytes say how much is held. For example, a recorder of 10 000 sensors holds 10 000 track definitions and passes them with each block. A block of 3 sensors' readings gets those 3 definitions and their clock written before it, once per 32 MiB plus one copy. A track added an hour in costs one record at its first block. A typed item that isn't a track's, such as a run record, is put with TapeWriter::put_item.

A caller's own block of definitions is refused. Definitions reach the file only as puts, so every definition in a file lies near the data that needs it.

Writing data¶

Data goes into the file in four forms:

  • Record blocks. Build one with BlockWriter: start it with the block's kind, its id (TapeWriter::next_id) and the clocks its records use, push records, then finish it and hand the bytes to TapeWriter::write_block, which gives back where it landed (Placed: its offset from the start of the piece and its len), or None for a block of puts, which lands as no single block of its own. Use record blocks for tracks that produce a few items at a time.
  • Chunks. Hand a chunk head and its columns to TapeWriter::write_chunk, which encodes the chunk block and gives back where it landed (Placed). PieceChain::write_chunk gives back a ChunkPlaced too: the same, with the seq of the piece it landed in, since a call may cut to a new piece first. PieceChain::write_block gives back nothing. Use chunks for tracks with many items.
  • Deferred blocks. Hand small blocks that may wait, such as summary blocks, to TapeWriter::defer_block. The writer puts their definitions at once. In an aligned file it places the block in the gap before a chunk's payload, at most DEFER_WINDOW (1 MiB) of writes later; in a file that isn't aligned it places the block at once.
  • State items. Put a state track's item with TapeWriter::put each time the producer uses it. It returns whether it wrote the item.

A state track takes its items only through puts. A caller's records on a state track in a block of data are refused, and so is an events chunk on a state track. A series track has no records: its samples go into series chunks as data, each chunk's head carrying its grid and scale, and a record, a put or an events chunk on it is refused.

The writer checks what it is handed in proportion to its cost. A chunk it encodes from columns has its head and column table checked, its track against the definitions and a series chunk's ticks against its grid; the payload is the caller's columns copied, and is not read back. A caller's whole block (write_block, adopt_block, defer_block) is read once with every CRC checked. Past that, each byte placed costs one pass more, the content hash. The writer doesn't compare a series chunk's grid with the chunk before it; a reader's SeriesTimeline does (Events and series).

Every CRC is a CRC-32C, computed with the CPU's CRC instructions where it has them (SSE4.2 on x86-64, the CRC extension of ARMv8) and by a table elsewhere, with the same result. just bench-chunks measures what the writer spends on a series chunk of 1 MiB (524 288 i16 samples). On an AMD EPYC Genoa virtual machine with 14 vCPUs at a load average of about 5, best of 5 rounds of 256 chunks:

What µs per chunk GB/s
chunk::write_chunk: copy and CRCs 53 19.82
Block::read: the CRCs checked 33 31.32
ItemHash::of: BLAKE3 213 4.92
TapeWriter::write_chunk from columns 254 4.12
chunk::write_chunk, then TapeWriter::write_block 281 3.73

The content hash is most of the cost: a writer that hands over columns spends about 41 µs per MiB besides it.

Puts, near and copies¶

A put writes its item unless the newest record of the item's slot is the same item, by its 128-bit BLAKE3 hash, less than the track's near behind. The distance is measured to where the data lands, after the puts in front of it. A put of an item that lies near still writes a copy while the piece holds fewer than 1 + copies records of it and the newest lies at least NearPolicy::copy_spacing (1 MiB) behind. Copies are written only at uses, never scheduled ahead.

Puts and copies go into blocks marked FLAG_REPEAT. Such a block carries the sequence number of the block before it and isn't counted, so the producer's sequence numbers stay its own count of its blocks. Puts that belong with the block after them are also marked FLAG_WITH_NEXT, which a resume uses (see below).

The writer remembers, per slot, where it last wrote what. That memory is capped (NearPolicy::memory_max) and forgets the slot written longest ago first. A forgotten slot costs one more write at its next put, never a wrong read. Numbers gives what puts and copies cost in a piece.

Aligned files¶

A file that holds series is usually aligned: a layout with an alignment of 4 KiB (Layout::ALIGNED, or TapeWriter::set_alignment(ww_tape::ALIGNMENT) before the first block) puts every chunk's sample payload on a 4 KiB boundary. A reader can then map it from the page cache or read it with direct I/O. Deferred blocks fill the space before an aligned payload while they fit, and zeros in the chunk's own head fill the rest. The summaries and checkpoints a series produces anyway fill most gaps, so alignment costs little. An event file isn't aligned; its blocks follow each other with nothing between them.

Syncing: what a crash can lose¶

Data the operating system hasn't written to the disk is lost in a power cut. The sync policy (SyncPolicy) bounds how much that can be:

SyncPolicy Syncs A crash loses at most
Interval(t), the default with t = 1 s when blocks wait and the last sync is t ago about t of data
EveryBlocks(n) after every n blocks n blocks
None only at the finish everything the system hasn't written yet

Interval acts when a block arrives. A producer whose input may stop for a while calls TapeWriter::tick now and then, so the last blocks are synced even when no new block comes; with IndexPolicy::interval set, tick also places a checkpoint when its time is up. In an unaligned file the checkpoint goes in at once; in an aligned file tick only marks it due, and it goes into the gap before the next chunk. TapeWriter::sync syncs at once.

A failed write or sync ends the file. The writer reports it and refuses every later write. It never retries, because after a failed fsync Linux may drop the pages it couldn't write, and a second fsync then succeeds without them.

Checkpoints¶

A reader of a file that was never finished has no tail to start from. So the writer places a checkpoint index block after about every 4 MiB of blocks (IndexPolicy). Each checkpoint lists the blocks placed since the one before and points back at it. A reader after a crash finds the newest checkpoint and scans only the stretch after it, at most about 4 MiB.

Checkpoints are placed by bytes, not by time, because a place chosen by the clock would make the file's bytes depend on when it was written. A producer that wants checkpoints by time as well sets IndexPolicy::interval. In an aligned file a checkpoint waits for the gap before the next chunk payload.

Cuts: ending one piece and starting the next¶

A cut ends the current piece and starts the next piece of the chain. Only a piece of a chain can be cut: a writer starts one with TapeWriter::begin_piece, which writes the piece head first in the file. TapeWriter::cut(next_path) places every deferred block, writes the final index, the finish and the tail, renames the file, and returns the finished piece (Finished) and a writer on the next piece. The next piece starts with a piece head and the layout and producer. Its definitions and state are put again at their first uses, since near is measured inside a piece.

PieceChain writes a whole chain and cuts by itself by a CutPolicy, which has three limits: bytes, chunks and interval, an age counted from the piece's start or its resume. A limit of 0 or None is off, and the first limit reached cuts. The chain checks the policy before each chunk, so a cut by policy never comes after the last chunk and never leaves a piece without data. The chain also cuts before any write that would need more state entries than the piece's index may hold (IndexPolicy::state_entries_max), and PieceChain::cut_now cuts at once.

Nothing waits for a cut. Durability comes from the sync policy and the checkpoints, which work inside a piece; the cut only bounds how large one file grows. A slow input, such as one log line a minute, is written in a record block as it comes, synced within the sync interval, and readable by a reader of the unfinished piece at once. Cutting by age is there for retention by file, and it makes the file's bytes depend on the clock.

Finishing¶

TapeWriter::finish ends the piece as complete. It places the deferred blocks, then writes the final index and the finish block, padded so the piece ends on a 4 KiB boundary, then the tail. It syncs, renames NAME.partial to NAME and syncs the directory. It returns Finished: the piece's name, its content hash, its length and its ids. finish_as ends the piece in another state, such as aborted. A file still named NAME.partial was never finished.

Relays¶

A relay is a program that takes a live producer's blocks and writes them into a tape of its own. For example, a host receives a device's tape blocks over a link and records them to disk, or a capture tool reads a live feed and keeps a file of it. The producer's stream has its own puts and copies, made by the producer's near rule in the producer's stream. They don't fit the relay's file: the relay's file may start later, be cut elsewhere, or have lost blocks.

TapeWriter::relay_block(&mut defs, block) passes such a stream on, one block at a time. A block of puts (marked FLAG_REPEAT) has its definitions taken into the relay's Definitions, and its state records are put on the relay's tape by the relay's own near rule (TapeWriter::put_block). Any other block is written as data under those definitions. The relay therefore needs no knowledge of the producer's tracks, and its file has every definition near its use like any other.

On a raw medium a piece must be one session, as Damage and recovery explains. A writer for raw media passes a producer's blocks with TapeWriter::adopt_block, which renumbers each block into the writer's own session and gives back where it landed (Placed), None for a block of puts. A jump in the producer's sequence numbers stays a jump in the writer's, so a reader still sees blocks that were lost on the way.

Resuming after a crash¶

TapeWriter::resume(path, options) reopens the unfinished piece of path, the file NAME.partial that TapeWriter::create(NAME) wrote, and goes on writing the same piece. It returns the writer and a Resumed, which says how many bytes it kept and dropped and where the piece was cut. It works in three steps:

  1. It finds the piece as a reader would: by its tail when it has one, else from the newest checkpoint and a scan after it, else by a scan from the start. A scan stops at the first bad block.
  2. It keeps every byte up to the end of the last good block. It cuts off the rest: a torn block, an end of piece, and blocks marked FLAG_WITH_NEXT at the end of what it keeps, since the block they belong with didn't survive.
  3. It replays the kept blocks to rebuild the writer as it was: the content hash, the index entries, the track ranges, the record of what lies near, the session and the next sequence number.

The producer then gives again every call from the block TapeWriter::next_seq reports, with the same definitions. Since what the writer adds depends only on the bytes before it, the calls and the options, the resumed piece ends with the same bytes, and the same content hash, as a piece written without the crash. A put is idempotent, so the producer simply puts again what it uses and needs no record of which puts reached the disk. The writer holds no definitions, so a resume needs none from the file: the application passes its own, as before the crash.

A resume checks the options against the piece before it cuts or writes anything. Pass the options the piece was written with; TapeWriter::options gives them. A layout or producer other than the newest the piece kept is refused (WriteError::Layout, WriteError::Producer), because the writer would place blocks the way the file says it doesn't. A piece found with its finish (a crash after the finish, before the rename) is opened again: its finish and tail are cut off, and the next finish pads the piece to 4 KiB again.

PieceChain::resume does the same for a chain. It reads the finished pieces back from their tails and resumes the last, or starts the next piece when the crash came between a cut's finish and the next piece's start. A chain whose last piece already ended complete or aborted is refused with WriteError::Ended. Damage and recovery follows a recorder through a power cut.

Same input, same bytes¶

A writer given the same input writes the same bytes. Blocks hold no wall time, puts and copies follow from the bytes before them, and checkpoints and cuts are placed by bytes. A producer that seals blocks on a timer, adds checkpoints by time or cuts pieces by age gives up this property for what it places that way. Tapes says what the property is good for.
